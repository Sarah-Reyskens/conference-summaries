# F*ck The System, and other bad ideas for the future

Jo Caudron · Closing Keynote · Agentic Engineering & Tooling

## TL;DR

Caudron argues the 80-year post-war order is over and pressure from the US is creating a unique momentum for Europe to build sovereign energy, technology, and industry. His program: copy back lost technology instead of rebuilding from scratch, compete through transitions rather than old paradigms, and quit the AI compute sprint in favor of a marathon of absorbing AI into the economy.

## Key takeaways

- European sovereignty stacks as a pyramid: energy first, then technology, then economy, then military, and only together do they produce agency.
- "Sovereign clouds" run by American companies cannot guarantee sovereignty because the US CLOUD Act and FISA overrule any promise made on European soil.
- Europe pays roughly 400 billion euros a year in license fees to US tech and holds 4 trillion in US tech equity; redirecting a quarter of either would revive the European ecosystem.
- Rebuilding lost tech costs less than feared because functions can be reverse-engineered at a fraction of the original R&D; the premium should be judged as return on sovereignty, not return on investment.
- Europe should leave the frontier-model sprint (it cannot win on compute) and run an absorption marathon, since weaker, cheaper models already cover most economic uses.
- Competing means betting on transitions: energy treated as improving technology rather than a commodity, mobility sold as usage rather than cars.

## Notes

### [00:50](https://youtu.be/wUyQmRriCng?t=50) A robot opens the closing keynote

A humanoid robot on stage, introduced as already replacing organizer Stefan Janssen, frames the question of the hour: what fast-moving technology means for Europe and the future its builders want. Caudron then takes over to talk about sovereignty beyond the digital kind.

### [02:54](https://youtu.be/wUyQmRriCng?t=174) The old normal is over

The 80 years since WWII brought emancipation, education, welfare, and mobility that did not exist before, and most of the audience mistakes this for permanent. Through a Buddhist mountain metaphor, Caudron says every curve ends and the current accumulation of simultaneous crises, which he calls a meta-storm, is the descent. COVID-era hopes for new roaring twenties never materialized; the choice is a self-fulfilling decline or building a next mountain.

### [09:08](https://youtu.be/wUyQmRriCng?t=548) Europe's Pearl Harbor moment

Caudron names the early-2025 Oval Office ambush of Zelensky as the symbolic rupture of the US-led rules-based order, comparable to Pearl Harbor in forcing a power to engage. He stresses this is policy, not rhetoric: the White House national security, cyber, and counterterrorism strategies literally name Europe a cultural adversary, so outlasting one president changes nothing.

### [12:15](https://youtu.be/wUyQmRriCng?t=735) Momentum: Europe pushes back

Within weeks Europe pledged 800 billion for resilience, France and the UK floated a joint nuclear umbrella, and Germany removed the constitutional debt brake to fund defense, infrastructure, and technology. The EU-Canada associate membership, with Australia asking to join, signals middle powers teaming up. Momentum is real, Caudron says, but worthless unless acted upon.

### [17:28](https://youtu.be/wUyQmRriCng?t=1048) The sovereignty pyramid

Arms alone do not save Europe. The pyramid runs energy first (self-sufficient models cut loose from decades of crises), then technology, then economy, then military, producing agency at the top: the leverage to say stop to any bullying power. Digital sovereignty itself splits into three distinct races: data/applications/cloud, AI, and platforms such as social media and YouTube.

### [19:33](https://youtu.be/wUyQmRriCng?t=1173) How Europe lost tech, and how tech became a weapon

Europe unlearned the path from invention to product through two paths of least resistance: founders raising money and going to market in the US, and manufacturing moving to China. Today over 80% of European applications, data, and cloud run on American platforms, with zero European social media of importance. Weaponization is already here: Musk switching off Starlink over Crimea, ICC judges cut off from Microsoft, Google, Apple, Visa, and Mastercard, and the 19-day "Fabless Five" cutoff that showed policymakers what a kill switch looks like.

### [26:50](https://youtu.be/wUyQmRriCng?t=1610) Sovereign clouds, EuroStack, and the public sector leading

Vendor pledges always say "we will help", never "we will guarantee", because US law overrules them. Europe's answer includes the EuroStack concept (resilience at every layer so a cutoff means switching, not stopping), the June technology-sovereignty package worth hundreds of billions, and public buyers leading: France banning Teams, Zoom, and Meet in public services by 2027 in favor of Visio, and German states requiring the open ODF document format.

### [32:34](https://youtu.be/wUyQmRriCng?t=1954) Follow the money, then copy it back

European companies pay about 400 billion euros yearly in license fees while European savings hold 4 trillion in US tech equity; a quarter of either redirected would transform the ecosystem. Catching up is cheaper than assumed: SpaceX cut launch costs by orders of magnitude and Chinese labs distill frontier models into near-frontier open weights. Since much of this knowledge originated in Europe, Caudron advocates "copying back", including reverse-engineering products like Facebook at a fraction of their original cost instead of rebuilding decades of code.

### [38:52](https://youtu.be/wUyQmRriCng?t=2332) Return on sovereignty

Rebuilding costs more, so business cases need a new KPI next to ROI: return on security, or return on sovereignty. Insurance logic justifies it: replacing hackable Chinese solar converters would cost Europe 50 billion, while a multi-day grid blackout like Spain's would cost hundreds of billions in damage. Danish e-buses hackable from China and Chinese cars banned from Polish military sites show the risk is concrete.

### [42:29](https://youtu.be/wUyQmRriCng?t=2549) Transition thinking beats competing

Europe cannot compete with old paradigms: not US tech, not Chinese manufacturing costs. It can win where industries transition anyway. Energy as a technology improves yearly (the solar chart shows efficiency climbing while price collapses since 2000), unlike oil, which stays oil. Mobility shifts from selling 45,000-euro cars to autonomous 24/7 fleets where build cost stops mattering because value is captured per ride.

### [47:11](https://youtu.be/wUyQmRriCng?t=2831) Quit the AI sprint, run the marathon

The "Europe 2031" essay says Europe is doomed unless it matches US compute (255 GW planned versus Europe's 21 GW). Caudron calls this a paradox: filling new gigafactories with American chips deepens the dependency that is now a liability. Frontier-model reasons (military dominance, economic dumping, superintelligence) all trigger mutually assured economic destruction if actually used. The marathon alternative: Europe's planned compute already covers running the continent on AI, and the benchmark chart shows cheap models (a highlighted option 38x cheaper and only 14 points lower on SWE-bench Verified) suffice for most economic uses, with the AI Act providing social stability US deployments lack.

### [56:01](https://youtu.be/wUyQmRriCng?t=3361) Loss aversion and Europe 2.0

Closing with psychology: loss aversion makes Europeans at the top of the curve fear losing everything, while those with nothing have perspective to grow. Placing energy, resources, technology, industry, defense, and climate adaptation at the foot of a new curve reframes them as an engineering adventure that gives the next generation hope. For the first time in recent history, Americans and Chinese are the incumbents and Europeans the challengers.

## Who should watch

Builders and policymakers who want arousing big-picture case for European tech sovereignty with concrete numbers and frameworks to reuse. Skip it if you want implementation detail on any single technology named; this is a manifesto keynote, and the AI compute debate is argued, not engineered.
