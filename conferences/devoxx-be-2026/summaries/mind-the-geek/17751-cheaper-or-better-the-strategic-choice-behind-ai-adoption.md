# Cheaper or Better? The Strategic Choice Behind AI Adoption

Miriam Sasse · Deep Dive · Mind the Geek

## TL;DR

Sasse, a transformation consultant and mechanical engineer, frames AI adoption as game design: leadership bets on using AI to cut costs (cheaper) or to create customer value (better), and that bet has to reshape roles, rules, metrics and processes instead of only speeding up the existing workflow. The session alternates short framework inputs with audience exercises collected on Padlet: system cards, strategy principles, AI modes, alpha players, gatekeepers, and CD-versus-streaming logic. It leaves a diagnostic toolkit for deciding where AI dominates decisions, who must be involved early, and what freed-up time should buy.

## Key takeaways

- State the AI bet as cheaper or better with priorities; "both" without priorities pushes strategic calls down to individual developers.
- Map the whole system (process, roles, rules, stakeholders, technology, market, society) before automating, since automating the wrong step produces tools experts refuse to touch.
- Match the AI mode to the team: assistance, integration, or agent; agent mode without new rules for contact rights, decisions, monitoring and kill switches creates unowned risk.
- Watch for AI as alpha player: suggestions accepted without scrutiny and a blurred line between recommendation and decision; counter it with role clarity, broad access, co-creation and visible impact.
- Involve the four gatekeepers (finance, IT, HR, compliance) from the start with what each one needs; constraints belong inside the game design, not at the end of it.
- Decide deliberately what saved time buys, because refilling it with more tickets recreates the loss through context switching (Weinberg: five parallel tasks cost 75% of time).
- Ask whether a process should exist at all (the status-report robot, CD versus streaming) instead of only making the old process faster.

## Notes

### [00:33](https://youtu.be/Ycdt7IT7Kdw?t=33) The "or" and the game-design lens

The most important word in the title is "or": cheaper or better, and whether a team can honestly pursue both. Sasse introduces herself as a mechanical engineer who studied in Japan, anime fan, and lead of deep-tech teams, now working on transformation and organizational design at S&N Invent. Her lens for the session is game design, the subject of her German book Level Up! (English edition expected about two months after the talk). The premise: if you cannot do organizational design, imagine the workplace as a game and ask whether you would like to play it. She shows a hand-painted "island of game design" with sections for structures, problems and irritations, emotions and motivation, team growth, and digitalization with AI. Two provocations set the tone: games need problems and negative emotions to stay interesting, while workplaces treat both as defects; and game designers were early AI integrators, learning from chess engines that an artificial opponent is most fun when only slightly stronger than the player.

### [09:35](https://youtu.be/Ycdt7IT7Kdw?t=575) Exercise: how AI rewrites the rules

The first audience exercise asks how much the workplace game has changed: new rules, roles, ways of managing. Answers go to a browser-based Padlet via QR code. In the review at [14:51](https://youtu.be/Ycdt7IT7Kdw?t=891), recurring themes are less implementation work and more review, nearly no handwritten code except after tool failure, more focus on design and context up front, and unease that seniority signals have lost value while capabilities shift almost monthly. Sasse notes that changed communication styles change culture, and promises the Padlet stays readable after the session.

### [17:35](https://youtu.be/Ycdt7IT7Kdw?t=1055) Cheaper or better: USAA and Schindler

The efficiency side means fewer people, less time, removed bottlenecks (with the warning that every removed bottleneck reveals the next). The customer-loyalty side means using data and AI to create attachment: the USAA bank example, which organizes around life phases such as marriage or retirement instead of bank accounts, so customers say they love their bank. The counter-example is Schindler elevators, using machine data for predictive maintenance: genuinely efficient, but nothing a customer falls in love with. A second exercise asks where the audience sits; most answers land on efficiency, some on both. Sasse warns that "both" with no priorities usually means no strategy, which leaves each developer to resolve the trade-off alone.

### [27:45](https://youtu.be/Ycdt7IT7Kdw?t=1665) Where to automate: CLAAS and the system card

Just because a step can be automated, it should not always be automated. At CLAAS (agricultural machinery), the team added checklists and an expert database to machines, but line workers ignored them: consulting it felt like admitting they were no longer experts. That was the wrong place to automate. The tool offered instead is the system card, borrowed from board-game design: the process from agreed start to end in the middle, surrounded by roles, rules, stakeholders and influencers, teams, technology, market and customer, and society. The worked example is a polymer-flow simulation company mapped six years earlier (40 people) and re-mapped recently (about 65): new roles and rules such as the AI Act marked with plus signs, vanished elements with minus signs, shifted process boundaries with arrows. A follow-up exercise collects the audience's own plus/minus changes, including "programmer minus, code reviewer plus", an "AI context manager" role, more QA, customers expecting three-week software, and one participant's coinage "meat proxy" for a human reduced to pressing buttons for the machine.

### [47:09](https://youtu.be/Ycdt7IT7Kdw?t=2829) Five principles of a usable strategy

Good strategy (1) contains no trivialities: slogans every competitor could print help nobody decide; (2) never makes the decision for employees but gives direction, such as "comfort beats price on travel so you arrive at full power" instead of "always take the train"; (3) is not a goal, illustrated by a "Strategy 800" that only named a revenue figure and said nothing about how to earn it; (4) stays tangible enough to argue about; (5) is an entrepreneurial bet whose opposite must also be conceivable, otherwise it decides nothing. These five apply directly to AI adoption choices.

### [54:00](https://youtu.be/Ycdt7IT7Kdw?t=3240) Three AI modes: assistance, integration, agent

Assistance mode means AI on demand and ad hoc (classifying tickets, analyzing reports); the organization stays unchanged. Integration mode puts AI on a fixed path: roles shift, someone validates output, exceptions need handling, interfaces need design, and AI becomes part of the game mechanics. Agent mode makes AI a team player, which requires redesigned mechanics: who the agent may contact, which decisions it can take, who monitors and stops it, what happens when two agents conflict. The framing is augmentation over substitution (machines bring speed and consistency, humans bring context, responsibility and judgment), and no mode ranks above the others: agent mode has to fit the team. Audience answers spread across all three, with several people uneasy about jumping to agent mode before writing the new rules, and some stuck in assistance mode awaiting management approval for anything further.

### [71:29](https://youtu.be/Ycdt7IT7Kdw?t=4289) Alpha players: diagnosis and counter-rules

Cooperative board games suffer from alpha players who know the game so well they end up playing alone while everyone else becomes a human interface. Board games counter this with no communication, traitors or spies, hidden cards (limited information), distributed abilities, and time limits. The transfer: AI must not become the alpha player. Four diagnostic questions, each with three checks, test whether AI dominates decisions (suggestions adopted faster than discussed, criticism seen as waste), expertise concentrates in a few specialists, role awareness is missing (unclear who verifies, interprets, corrects; people feeling like clickers; recommendation and decision blurring), and access is narrow (complex tools, experiments only where tech resources sit, "I may only use it"). Countermeasures are role awareness, accessibility, co-creation over command, and transparent impact rather than transparent logic. Audience reports include agents silently fixing things they were only asked to investigate, freedom creeping in through vague prompts, and hidden personal AI use nobody else can audit; remedies named are sandboxes, strict boundaries, and AI enablement for every developer.

### [90:42](https://youtu.be/Ycdt7IT7Kdw?t=5442) The four gatekeepers

After the break, Sasse introduces the four gatekeepers of AI transformation as game characters: the knight (finance: "we have no money"), the samurai (IT: "technically impossible"), the amazon (HR: "our people lack the skills"), and the cyborg (compliance and risk: "too risky"). They are not villains; they have a function, and the whole game must be designed around them from the start instead of colliding with them at the end. The exercise asks how people involve them early. Answers include joint policy-making with decision makers, capped pilot-team budgets that grow on demonstrated improvement, and honest admissions of ignoring gatekeepers to pass the point of no return. Each gatekeeper needs something specific: finance wants limited investment plus a feedback loop of success stories, architecture wants boundaries, HR wants a learning path, compliance wants traceability and human decision points. The section closes on constraints: games are fun because moves and resources are limited (the Yahtzee example: five dice, fixed combinations), so the best AI experiments are the constrained ones.

### [108:29](https://youtu.be/Ycdt7IT7Kdw?t=6509) Old game or new game: the robot, the music industry, CD versus streaming

A friendly robot generating beautiful status reports prompts the uncomfortable question: if AI writes the same report faster, the old game improved, but would continuous information, emerging-risk detection and built-in feedback remove the need for the report at all. Digital transformation is not putting a robot in front of an old process. The music industry illustrates the difference: recording, then the CD as a digital product inside an unchanged analog game (artist, label, factory, retailer, owner), then streaming as a genuinely new game (ownership to access, retailers gone, platforms and recommendation algorithms as gatekeepers), with AI-generated personalized music possibly the next rewrite. The CD-logic versus streaming-logic checklist (five boxes each, read off the slide) contrasts thinking in processes, efficiency-located value, planning and control, "integrating AI", and knowing all rules first, against resonance-located value, data shaping decisions, dynamic roles, fast experimentation, and feedback without full visibility. Audience reactions include "the new game is already here", blurrier roles and a shifting definition of done, AI-generated tests for AI-generated code undermining test value, and agile routines drifting toward Kanban under faster feedback. A Munchkin analogy warns that rules must not change faster than they get used. An agile aside observes that hardware and physics teams failed at agile at exactly the rigid points (monolithic structures, no small slices or feedback loops) that now block their AI adoption too.

### [131:57](https://youtu.be/Ycdt7IT7Kdw?t=7917) What saved time buys: Weinberg, grey gentlemen, dice

Efficiency is valuable until it becomes the definition of value: a team saving 20% of its time usually just receives 20% more tickets. Worse, parallel tickets multiply context-switching loss, quoted from Gerald Weinberg as 20% for two tasks, 40% for three, 60% for four, and 75% for five. The strategic question is what the released capacity, time and intelligence get spent on. One prop is a paper dice to throw each morning: empty slot (a 10-minute gap for doing nothing), meaning check ("why am I doing this?"), human touch, slow move, genuine connection, Momo minute. The grey gentlemen from Michael Ende's Momo, who talk people into "saving" time and then smoke it in cigars, stand for AI that absorbs every efficiency gain until people feel they have less time than before. Final audience answers include never pasting raw AI output on another human, using AI for structure rather than speed, letting AI draft documentation alongside code changes for human verification, reconnecting with colleagues, and keeping 30 to 60 disconnected minutes a day. The closing lines: speed alone is no strategy, automation is no transformation, productivity is not value; design the whole game. Contact runs through LinkedIn and email, with the Level Up! book and an AI-in-project-management booklet on the final slide.

## Who should watch

Team leads, managers, agile coaches and architects who shape AI adoption or organizational change, and anyone asked to choose between efficiency plays and value plays. Skip it for code, model internals, benchmarks or measured ROI cases: this is a strategy workshop with facilitated exercises, and long stretches are silent group work summarized afterward rather than speaker input.

## Verdict

**5/10**: Consulting experience grounds an AI-adoption workshop with usable diagnostic questions, but choosing a strategy remains largely an exercise for the viewer and the quoted figures come without a method.

**Reach segment**: Tech leads, architects and coaches driving AI adoption in business-application teams can use the diagnostic toolkit; most developers only feel its effects secondhand.
