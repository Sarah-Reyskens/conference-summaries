# rm -rf self-doubt: Debugging Impostor Syndrome for Developers
Vincent Mayers · Conference · People, Practices & Career

## TL;DR
Impostor syndrome is a normal threat response, not a verdict on competence: the brain's fight-or-flight circuitry fires at code reviews and new roles the way it fires at physical danger. Mayers traces his own path from a pottery degree to developer relations, explains the neuroscience, and offers practices for turning self-doubt into growth instead of self-condemnation.

## Key takeaways
- "I don't understand this code yet" is information about a knowledge gap; "therefore I shouldn't be here" turns it into a verdict on identity. The goal is to stop that conversion.
- A PR with 37 comments or a message from a senior triggers the amygdala's fight-or-flight response even though nothing physically threatening happened; the jaw tightening and cold hands are adrenaline and cortisol doing their job.
- Anticipate the response instead of waiting for it to pass: competing athletes and experienced speakers still feel it every time and perform by leaning into it.
- Review the work, never the person: a pile of PR comments signals a solvable knowledge gap, and seniors who admit what they don't know build teams that handle this better.
- AI output that "mostly works" exposes a genuine knowledge gap you can bridge; it does not invalidate years of judgment about context, trade-offs and responsibility.
- Impostor syndrome is not a medical diagnosis and needs no cure; treat it as a feature to understand and harness rather than a bug to squash.

## Notes

### [01:07](https://youtu.be/Tfrt19d3TEA?t=67) From pottery to technology
Mayers opens with his own contradiction: he holds a degree in pottery, pivoted from a planned civil-engineering path after a miserable year retaking maths under a hostile teacher, then worked as an athlete, designer, salesperson, grocery manager and tech recruiter. Recruiting brought him from England to the US and into inBloom (the Gates-funded education-data nonprofit building a secure data service for US public schools) as a community manager, which led to developer-relations roles at Oracle, JFrog, Gradle and Neo4j, plus running the Atlanta Java User Group and DevNexus. He felt like Anakin before the Jedi Council facing the engineers, and still feels like an impostor despite the evidence of the path. The point: careers in technology have side doors, and you decide whether earlier experience counts.

### [07:28](https://youtu.be/Tfrt19d3TEA?t=448) What impostor syndrome is
The term comes from Clance and Imes's 1978 paper "The Impostor Phenomenon in High Achieving Women": the internal experience of believing you are less competent than evidence shows. Four common symptoms, each with a developer-flavored thought: persistent self-doubt ("I got this promotion because they couldn't find anyone else"), attribution of success to luck or timing ("they only invited me to speak because someone else cancelled"), fear of exposure ("if they saw how much I use AI, they'd realize I'm not that good"), and dismissal of positive feedback ("the bottleneck was obvious, anyone could have fixed it").

### [10:03](https://youtu.be/Tfrt19d3TEA?t=603) Normal, and the self-doubt distinction
Einstein ("I feel compelled to regard myself as an involuntary swindler"), Michelle Obama and Serena Williams all describe the feeling, so its presence says nothing about competence. The key distinction: "I don't understand this code yet" states a fixable gap, while "I don't understand this code, therefore I shouldn't be on this team" condemns the person. The objective is to eliminate the conversion of doubt into self-condemnation. He then presents the "impostor CI pipeline": unfamiliar task, self-doubt, panic with 2 a.m. overpreparation or PR avoidance, merged success, rejection of the evidence ("I only succeeded because I worked twice as long"), and repeat.

### [13:11](https://youtu.be/Tfrt19d3TEA?t=791) Fight or flight, with Glenn
Perceived threats to self-image trigger the same circuitry as physical danger. Meet Glenn, a software engineer whose amygdala senses danger and signals the hypothalamus, which fires the sympathetic nervous system; the adrenal glands release adrenaline within seconds (raised heart rate, faster breathing, cold hands as blood leaves the extremities), followed by cortisol, the stress hormone that sustains the response. Adrenaline is the pager alert; cortisol is the long-running incident process. The on-screen trigger: a GitHub PR ("Refactor self-belief engine #42", 37 comments, changes requested, merge blocked) plus Slack messages from someone senior. Common triggers include competition, speaking on stage (Sheryl Sandberg reports impostor syndrome every time) and explaining work to a principal engineer. Mayers's own version: every whitewater-kayaking start line, and a first-ever talk that was a keynote to 4,000 people at Oracle Code One.

### [19:29](https://youtu.be/Tfrt19d3TEA?t=1169) The AI angle
AI gave impostor thinking new vocabulary: "if the machine can produce this, what am I for?" But engineering was never just producing syntax; judgment, context, trade-offs, responsibility and communication still belong to people. Quoting a viral Andrej Karpathy post about vibe-coded projects that "mostly work", he argues that "mostly works" is not good enough and that AI exposing a knowledge gap is actionable information: do not turn a temporary gap into an identity verdict.

### [21:32](https://youtu.be/Tfrt19d3TEA?t=1292) Practices for overcoming it
Impostor syndrome can be useful when it drives healthy self-questioning and learning; lean into the fight response rather than flight. Reframe weaknesses as strengths (his impatience makes him efficient). Validate the feelings and expect them, in the spirit of the Henry Ford quote from the opening: "whether you think you can or you think you can't, you're right." Celebrate small wins, since even showing up can be hard for reasons you cannot see. Develop a healthy response to failure: Kellogg's corn flakes came from a ruined batch of boiled oats left overnight, and Netflix's outage response became chaos engineering. Challenge perfectionism, eat the elephant one bite at a time, and stop comparing your life to other people's highlight reels on social media.

### [27:22](https://youtu.be/Tfrt19d3TEA?t=1642) Speaker lessons and team dynamics
At a JFokus speaker unconference session on impostor syndrome, the group concluded: accept you will never be the smartest person in the room, you need knowledge and passion rather than expertise, and if one person takes one thing from your talk the job is done (that thing is usually a reminder of something basic, like doing more unit testing). On teams: systemic bias, exclusion, microaggressions and prestige cultures intensify the feeling, and the feeling can come from the environment rather than from inside. If you lead people, review the work rather than the person, and make uncertainty visible: seniors who admit ignorance build better teams than those on mountaintops.

### [33:06](https://youtu.be/Tfrt19d3TEA?t=1986) Takeaways
Impostor syndrome is a coined term, not a diagnosis; there are no pills for it. It is your brain's fault, an illusion of your own making, universal and manageable. Treat it as a feature to understand, harness the energy for growth, and lean into discomfort as a growth mechanism, illustrated with a photo of Navy SEAL training. Closing armor: nobody else in the world is you, and that unique perspective is worth sharing.

### [35:10](https://youtu.be/Tfrt19d3TEA?t=2110) Q&A
Asked how to tell impostor syndrome from actually not being good, Mayers says failing a task signifies a knowledge gap that can close quickly with effort (his daughter's retaken biology test), never a verdict on belonging. Asked for tips when the body plays tricks, he says the feeling never went away for him in competition or for Sandberg on stage; anticipate it and lean in. The short and long answer to "how do you get over it" is "just get over it", with the nuance that getting over it means recognizing and working with it.

## Who should watch
Developers who dread code reviews, hesitate to speak up in design discussions or feel fraudulent stepping into larger roles will get the most, especially those early in their careers or entering through non-traditional paths. Team leads get concrete review and culture practices. Skip it if you want clinical psychology or data on interventions; this is lived experience plus popular neuroscience, not research.
