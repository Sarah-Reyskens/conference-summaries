# Coder, Coach, Catalyst - using questions to make people grow

Martin Mazur · Conference · People, Practices & Career

## TL;DR

Senior technologists who solve every problem brought to them become overloaded heroes, then uncooperative bottlenecks, while the people around them never learn. Mazur's answer is basic coaching technique: active listening plus a five-question conversation structure that helps people find their own solutions. The talk walks the full framework through acted-out dialogues between a tech lead and a developer.

## Key takeaways

- Stop taking ownership of problems people bring you: solving everything for them keeps them dependent and turns you into the team bottleneck.
- Open coaching moments with "What's on your mind?", then dig with repeated "And what else?" until the stated problem gives way to the actual one.
- Always ask "What is the real challenge here for you?", with the "for you": without it people spiral into things outside their control.
- When someone asks for a decision or advice, build a reusable heuristic with them (criteria they can apply next time) instead of giving the answer.
- Never coach through genuine psychological issues (burnout, depression, worse): stop, and actively help the person reach professional support.
- Use a permission question ("Would you be open to talking through this?") before coaching someone above you in the hierarchy.

## Notes

### [00:34](https://youtu.be/yhk1qvy6A_4?t=34) The promise: one skill, three formats, five questions

Mazur, a consultant and coach with 20 years in software, frames the talk as a starter kit: active listening, three coaching formats, and five groups of questions. Using them changes how you listen, how you speak, and how others perceive you. He warns the formula is simple but the application is not, and that value only comes from practice.

### [03:13](https://youtu.be/yhk1qvy6A_4?t=193) The hero-to-villain trap

Technologists are rewarded for producing correct answers, so when colleagues bring problems they take them on ("leave it with me and I'll fix it") and become appreciated heroes. Past a point the hero is overburdened and either quits or burns out, or turns villain: snarky, uncooperative, a black hole where work disappears. Standard managerial advice (just say no, delegate more, set boundaries, care less, clone yourself) is mocked as unhelpful. There are two problems: the "you" problem of overload, and the longer-term "them" problem, where relieved-of-everything colleagues never develop their own skills.

### [09:25](https://youtu.be/yhk1qvy6A_4?t=565) What coaching means here

The definition comes from tennis coach Timothy Gallwey: pointing out where the error is, without giving the answer, produces lasting self-correction, while direct instruction only fixes the moment. Coaching here means helping others discover the solution through questions, working on the person rather than the problem, for long-term growth rather than immediate payoff, adapted to the individual instead of one-size-fits-all.

### [13:02](https://youtu.be/yhk1qvy6A_4?t=782) Active listening, the one skill

Everything hinges on active listening: being fully present and attending to words, tone, body language, and situation, instead of waiting for your turn to speak. Tactics include eye contact, verbal cues and nodding, and paraphrasing back (which both proves you listened and lets the speaker correct misunderstandings). Underneath it all is empathy, defined as accepting what you hear as the speaker's truth even when you disagree. A practice exercise for teams: one person states a problem, the other may only ask "Why is that?", "What was that about?", or "How was that for you?", which frees the brain from planning the next question. This is a root-cause-analysis drill, not coaching itself.

### [18:15](https://youtu.be/yhk1qvy6A_4?t=1095) Three coaching formats

Formal coaching means booked 45-minute sessions with an agreed coach/coachee relationship, working toward long-term behavioral change across sessions; hard for beginners and rarely available to individual contributors. Impromptu (situational, on-the-fly) coaching is the most useful format: 5-to-15-minute bite-sized conversations at a desk, in a meeting, or by the coffee machine, focused on one situation, needing no permission or agreement. Done well it visibly changes how people perceive you and leads to formal engagements. Team coaching addresses whole-team behavior change in one session, which avoids suspicion and saves time, but requires mode-switching between coach, facilitator, and mediator when people disagree.

### [24:31](https://youtu.be/yhk1qvy6A_4?t=1471) The five questions, part 1: initiation and excavation

The five groups (initiation, excavation, focus, options, action) are demonstrated through a fictitious dialogue between tech lead Taylor and developer Sam, who arrives stressed about last-minute requirement changes. The favorite initiation question is "What's on your mind?"; avoid "How are you?" (emotionally loaded), yes/no questions (nothing to work with), status-sounding questions ("How's it going?"), and prompting for problems ("Do you have any problems?"), which manufactures low-priority issues. Excavation digs past the first-stated problem: the best tool is silence, the practical one is repeating "And what else?" until it gets awkward or circular. Sam's deadline worry turns out to involve not pushing back on changes as a new joiner.

### [30:12](https://youtu.be/yhk1qvy6A_4?t=1812) Part 2: focus, options, action

The focus question, "What is the real challenge here for you?", keeps the problem inside the person's circle of control; the "for you" is essential. Sam surfaces imposter syndrome: feeling they should handle everything as the new person. Option questions explore the space without giving answers ("What have you tried so far?", "What worked in similar situations?", "What resources do you have?"), preserving the coachee's agency and accountability; Sam already tried longer hours and priority lists, so suggesting those would have failed. The action question closes: blunt ("What are you going to do about it?") or mild ("What's the smallest thing you can do now?"). Mazur picks a mild one given Sam's stress, and Sam commits to requesting a priorities sync with the product team by end of day. Accept their solution even if you would choose differently; if it fails they come back and the long-term work continues.

### [38:28](https://youtu.be/yhk1qvy6A_4?t=2308) Coaching decisions with heuristics

Giving answers to advice-seekers ties rubber bands around people who all snap back with the next question. Instead, build a heuristic (rule of thumb) for the class of problem: examples are the military's "keep moving, seek the high ground, stay in touch" and the Agile manifesto's "individuals and interactions over processes and tools". In a second dialogue Sam asks "React or Vue?"; Taylor elicits Sam's criteria (ease of use, community support), adds missing ones (scalability, licensing) by briefly breaking coaching mode, confirms Sam can gather the information and decide, and Sam leaves equipped for the next similar choice.

### [42:37](https://youtu.be/yhk1qvy6A_4?t=2557) Who can coach, and coaching up

No license is needed, and you can coach domains you know nothing about, since the answers come from the coachee; curiosity and good questions suffice. Coaching upward is awkward because of the power structure, so use a permission question first ("Would you be open to talking through this? I might have a few questions that could provide some perspective"), ideally one-on-one rather than in the meeting, and accept no gracefully.

### [45:16](https://youtu.be/yhk1qvy6A_4?t=2716) Four pitfalls

Real psychological issues (burnout, depression, eating disorders, suicidal thoughts) end the coaching immediately: be a good colleague and actively get the person professional help. A "shoulder to cry on" who only wants to vent gets the gentle "What do you need from me here?"; if they just want listening, listen without coaching questions. Gossip about an absent Ted is deflected with "What did Ted say when you talked to them about it?", which usually sends the person off to do exactly that. Third-person blame ("I can't deliver because test/platform...") is snapped back with the focus question until the problem is phrased as the speaker's own challenge.

### [49:55](https://youtu.be/yhk1qvy6A_4?t=2995) Recap

Active listening underpins everything; impromptu coaching is the format to start with; the five question groups run initiation, excavation, focus, options, action. Mazur closes with Peter Block: "Questions are more transforming than answers. The skill is getting the question right."

## Who should watch

Tech leads, senior engineers, and mentors around year ten-plus who feel overloaded by being everyone's solver, and anyone moving into a role where the work is suddenly about people. Skip it if you already practice structured coaching conversations (the framework will feel familiar) or if you want clinical or organizational-change depth rather than a starter kit.
