# Help! I hate my team mate!

Jessica Siewert · Conference · People, Practices & Career

## TL;DR

Team conflict is common, costly, and usually resolvable while it is still rational: Siewert pairs Dutch workplace statistics and standard conflict models with two anonymized cases from her own career. The session leaves you with five practices for getting out of a conflict and three for staying ahead of one, all aimed at teams where everyone means well.

## Key takeaways

- Intervene while a conflict is still rational: once people take stances it drifts toward win-lose, and escalation leaves only losers.
- Agree on the shared goal first; two people who both want stability and quality have enough common ground to hear each other out.
- Value the traits you lack: teams of only starters finish nothing, and teams of only theorists overengineer everything.
- Never vent about a teammate to a third party: listeners will assume you will talk about them the same way.
- Deliberately build trust before conflict arises: real team kickoffs, compliment exercises, and yearly share-what-I-bring sessions.
- Conflicts with structural bad intent need a confidential advisor, not peer techniques; this talk covers only good-intent situations.

## Notes

### [00:34](https://youtu.be/sWP9osLV45I?t=34) Opening and tribute to Margaret Hamilton

After opening the late-Thursday slot, Siewert honors Margaret Hamilton: MIT and NASA in the 1960s, coiner of "software engineering", author of the Apollo 11 flight software, and 2016 Presidential Medal of Freedom recipient. She introduces herself as a Scala, Java and Kotlin developer, occasional developer/scrum master, now consulting in the Dutch energy transition. The session plan: conflict theory, two anonymized interactive cases voted on via Mentimeter, then a wrap-up with five ways out of conflict and three ways to stay ahead of it.

### [08:37](https://youtu.be/sWP9osLV45I?t=517) Why this topic: the numbers

Dutch research (sources credited on the slide: TNO 2018 working-conditions survey, HBR on team conflict types) shows managers in large organizations spend about 20% of their time, roughly a day a week, managing team conflicts. Some 20 to 30% of Dutch employees experience a conflict they are involved in each year, and at least 70,000 people call in sick yearly because of a workplace conflict, a lower bound since short sick leave needs no stated reason.

### [10:44](https://youtu.be/sWP9osLV45I?t=644) Scope and the open door: talk before it breaks

Intentions and perceptions differ, and the talk excludes situations of obvious structural bad intent, which need a confidential advisor. The "open door" of the presentation: prevent conflicts by discussing values, opinions and differences calmly before anything arises, which urgency-driven team assembly usually skips. Investing in the relationship means suspending prejudgment, which Siewert calls deeply unnatural; when frustrated she asks herself whether she is prejudging from past experience. Reaching out early to an independent person (scrum master, agile coach) beats waiting.

### [13:56](https://youtu.be/sWP9osLV45I?t=836) The five phases of a conflict

Conflicts move through (1) unawareness, where at least one party does not know it is arising; (2) the rational phase, the easiest exit point and still winnable for both sides; (3) taking stances, the emotional phase that drifts toward win-lose; (4) escalation, a losers-only situation; (5) resolution or non-resolution. Knowing which phase you are in determines what you can still do.

### [17:08](https://youtu.be/sWP9osLV45I?t=1028) People differ: communication styles and team roles

Siewert dislikes boxing people in but finds models like DISC, Insights and Management Drives useful: everyone is a blend that shifts over time, yet knowing her own "blue" style helped her choose a reformer-led team over one with a dominant leader. Complementary pairs matter more: theoretical versus pragmatic, starter versus finisher, facilitating versus directing. She once disliked whichever side she was not; now she sees a good team needs the mix.

### [21:57](https://youtu.be/sWP9osLV45I?t=1317) Case 1: open clash with a teammate

Eight years ago at a 20-to-30-person SaaS company where uptime was everything, Siewert fixed a 5 a.m. production incident caused by a junior teammate's mistake, then clashed in front of the whole company with the colleague newly responsible for customer support and SLAs over whether to debrief company-wide immediately or via the team first. He overruled her. Audience votes split between talking to the teammate (14), the team lead (16), or getting another job (14). What she actually did: nothing for a day (she later learned she is an introvert), then talked it out the next day at her tech lead's prompting. It ended well because both shared the same goal, quality and stability, which gave them enough to listen to each other's perspectives; they worked together four more years.

### [28:53](https://youtu.be/sWP9osLV45I?t=1733) Case 2: a teammate who seems to underperform

During COVID, fully remote on a new team, Siewert noticed a colleague repeating the same standup update for weeks, never opening a pull request, dodging calls and deflecting help. The dilemma she highlights is the thin line between helping, reporting and gossiping when you do not know the cause. She tried direct support, then asked the scrum master whether she was the only one noticing; told he was getting support, she backed off, checked once more after three months, and stayed out. He eventually left by his own decision. Her takeaway: integrity and constructiveness are everything, and it is fine never to learn what was going on.

### [35:51](https://youtu.be/sWP9osLV45I?t=2151) Wrap-up: five ways out, three ways ahead

Five ways to get out of a conflict: (1) restart from the shared goal while things are still rational; (2) see each other's strengths, and protect each other from each other's weaknesses, which beats merely combining technical skills; (3) when camps or merged teams collide, drop both entrenched ways and find a third way both can accept; (4) communicate with integrity: no venting to buddies, and consider running blunt feedback through an LLM tuned to the recipient's culture (Indian, British, Egyptian) before sending; (5) be willing to learn from someone you dislike, who is usually good at something you are bad at.

Three ways to stay ahead: run real team kickoffs that go the extra mile on values and trust (illustrated by the baseball coach who made an average Dutch team world champions: "hard work can beat talent if talent doesn't work hard"); run a compliment exercise where everyone draws a teammate's name from an envelope and returns after lunch with a sincere compliment; and sit down yearly to share what each person brings and wants to learn. She closes by repeating that bad-intent situations need a trusted advisor, and the audience's own takeaways ("value what others bring", "search for common aspirations") confirm the message landed.

## Tools, libraries and specs

- Mentimeter: live audience polling used for the interactive case votes and closing takeaways.

## Who should watch

Developers and team members who currently grate on a colleague, or leads who want peer-level language for friction in good-intent teams. Skip it if you need HR process for harassment or structural misconduct, or a deep treatment of cross-cultural collaboration, both explicitly out of scope.

## Verdict

**6/10**: First-hand cases from the speaker's career and a usable exit-and-prevention framework, limited by textbook models and cited-but-unexamined statistics.

**Novelty 1/2**

- **+** [21:57](https://youtu.be/sWP9osLV45I?t=1317) The SaaS production-incident clash is a first-hand case with its plain resolution: a next-day conversation grounded in a shared goal.
- **+** [28:53](https://youtu.be/sWP9osLV45I?t=1733) The remote underperformance case names a dilemma no checklist resolves: the line between helping, reporting and gossiping when the cause is unknown.
- **·** [13:56](https://youtu.be/sWP9osLV45I?t=836) The five conflict phases and the DISC, Insights and Management Drives models are standard material, presented without original results.

**Demonstrated 1/2**

- **·** [21:57](https://youtu.be/sWP9osLV45I?t=1317) A people-skills talk with nothing to run; the live Mentimeter votes are audience interaction, not a demo of the claims.

**Measured 1/2**

- **+** [08:37](https://youtu.be/sWP9osLV45I?t=517) Conflict costs are quantified from named Dutch sources: 20% of manager time, 20-30% of employees affected yearly, 70,000 sick leaves as a lower bound.
- **+** [20:16](https://youtu.be/sWP9osLV45I?t=1216) The conflict-type shares (35% two people, 20-25% one individual, 20% subgroups) from the same research decide which two cases the talk covers.
- **−** [09:09](https://youtu.be/sWP9osLV45I?t=549) The figures are hedged ("let's say roughly", "reportedly") with no method, sample or baseline beyond noting the range depends on sector.

**Depth 2/2**

- **+** [15:01](https://youtu.be/sWP9osLV45I?t=901) The five-phase model explains why timing matters: rational disagreement is still win-win, stances drift to win-lose, escalation leaves only losers.
- **+** [40:32](https://youtu.be/sWP9osLV45I?t=2432) The no-venting advice carries its mechanism: short-term relief now teaches every listener you will talk about them next.
- **−** [38:59](https://youtu.be/sWP9osLV45I?t=2339) Dropping both camps' ways for a third way arrives as "in my experience it works best", with no account of how to get there.

**Reusable 1/2**

- **+** [46:58](https://youtu.be/sWP9osLV45I?t=2818) The envelope compliment exercise is specified end to end: names in an envelope, draw one, return after lunch with a sincere compliment.
- **·** [35:51](https://youtu.be/sWP9osLV45I?t=2151) The five ways out and three ways ahead, including the LLM prompt for a recipient's culture [42:09], are described practices with no published checklist or artifact.
